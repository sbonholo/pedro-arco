Arco

Bowed strings only

Cello, violin, viola, double bass and string orchestra. Nothing here is recorded and nothing is a sampled instrument: each string is simulated from its physics as you press it — a wave running to the bridge and back, with the bow gripping and slipping hundreds of times a second. Press a title and it sings.

Silent

Each string is a digital waveguide: a delay loop carrying the wave to the bridge and back, a stick-slip friction curve where the hair meets it, and a damping filter deciding how fast the harmonics die. Nothing about the tone is drawn by hand — the harmonics emerge from the loop and shift as the string moves, which is exactly what stacked oscillators cannot do and why they always sound like a synthesizer. Bow position, string damping and the body resonances of the box differ per instrument. Four-note chords roll, because no bow crosses four strings at once.

Above the physics sits the playing. Each line is split into phrases and shaped — a swell toward the peak, weight on the higher notes, a taper at the close. Bowing harder is brighter as well as louder, but that is not a filter trick: more bow really does put more energy into the upper harmonics. Vibrato shortens the string the way a finger does. Slurred notes carry the pitch between them. Endings broaden. Scales are exempt from all of it, because a scale is a measurement.

The repertoire is public domain. Themes are transcribed and arranged for one to three synthesized voices, not reproduced from an edition — scales and the Pachelbel ground are exact; the concerto and aria themes are readings. The Elgar is a concert arrangement of all four movements rather than the complete score, which runs near half an hour, and it is played in du Pré’s manner rather than neutrally: her enormous dynamic range, her wide and immediate vibrato, her audible slides, and above all her rubato, which stretches and drives the pulse the whole way through instead of merely easing the last bars. No performer’s recording appears here — not hers and not anyone’s. The ten players are named for the piece each is bound to, played in this app’s own voice.
